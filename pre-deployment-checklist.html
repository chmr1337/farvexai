Check 1 of 5 — access control

Users can only reach records they own

What it means

Authentication proves who you are. Authorization decides what you may open.

Every protected endpoint has to confirm the caller actually owns the record being requested, normally by matching the record's owner ID against the signed-in user's ID.

Why it matters before you ship

The failure has a name: an insecure direct object reference, usually shortened to IDOR. It is the most common serious bug we find in web applications.

If /api/orders/123 hands back the order without checking who asked, anyone can walk the ID range and read other customers' data.

Random UUIDs make guessing harder, but a UUID is not an authorization check. You still need an explicit ownership test on every request.

        Check 2 of 5 — account recovery
        
Reset links expire, and work exactly once

      


      

        

          

            
What it means

            
A reset link carries a single-use token. That token needs a short lifetime — fifteen to sixty minutes is normal — and must stop working the moment it is used or a newer one is issued.

          


          

            
Why it matters before you ship

            
Reset links land in mailboxes. Mailboxes get forwarded, screenshotted, left open on shared machines and turn up in breach dumps.

            
A token with no expiry is a permanent back door into the account. A short lifetime shrinks that window from forever down to minutes.

        Check 3 of 5 — untrusted input
        
Treat every value from the client as hostile

      


      

        

          

            
What it means

            

              Assume everything the client sends is hostile.
              Validate types, lengths and formats before anything else runs.
            

            

              Use parameterised queries or an ORM so input can never change the shape of a SQL statement, and escape anything rendered back into a page so an injected
              <script>
              tag cannot run in somebody else's browser.
            

          


          

            
Why it matters before you ship

            
SQL injection can read or drop an entire database. Cross-site scripting can steal a session and become account takeover.

            
Both have sat in the OWASP Top 10 for over a decade because they keep working, usually on the one field somebody forgot.

        Check 4 of 5 — cross-origin access
        
The API answers your own domains only

      


      

        

          

            
What it means

            

              CORS, or cross-origin resource sharing, tells browsers which sites may call your API.
              In production that list holds your own domains — not a wildcard, not localhost, not whatever was convenient while developing.
            

          


          

            
Why it matters before you ship

            
A wide-open policy lets a malicious site make authenticated calls to your API from a victim's browser, leaking data or acting on that person's behalf.

            

              Teams ship
              Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *
              out of a development config more often than anyone would like, and usually notice only once something abuses it.
            

          

        


        

          
          

            CORS is a browser protection.
            It does nothing about server-to-server requests. Authentication and authorization still do the real work.

        Check 5 of 5 — request volume
        
Expensive endpoints need a ceiling

      


      

        

          

            
What it means

            
Cap how many requests one client — identified by IP address, account or API key — can make inside a time window.

            
Put tighter limits on the expensive and abusable routes: sign-in, password reset, sign-up, search, and anything that sends email or SMS.

          


          

            
Why it matters before you ship

            
Without a ceiling, four things are on the table:

            
    
              
  • 
                
                Brute force against sign-in and password-reset endpoints.
              
    • 
              
  • 
                
                Credential stuffing run at the scale of a leaked password list.
              
    • 
              
  • 
                
                Denial of service from one noisy client, deliberate or not.
              
    • 
              
  • 
                
                A surprise cloud bill when something loops your costliest route overnight.
